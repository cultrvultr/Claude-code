# Episode 1 — A Valley Written in Limestone
*Listen on the plane. Part one of three. Approx. 30 minutes.*

> Production note: this is the geology and deep-time episode — why the caves exist, why the Vézère valley became the best address in Ice Age Europe, and who was living there for the last four hundred thousand years. Feed everything below the SCRIPT marker to the TTS, stopping at the pronunciation table.

**SCRIPT**

Mike. Emily. Welcome aboard.

Somewhere below you, the Atlantic is sliding past at five hundred miles an hour, and at the far end of this flight there's a wedding waiting for you at a château surrounded by apple orchards. But between you and that wedding, you two have quietly booked yourselves into something much stranger and much older than a party. You are going to the Vézère valley — say it vay-ZAIR — a modest green river valley in southwest France that happens to contain the densest concentration of prehistoric art anywhere on Earth. Fifteen sites in that one valley are inscribed by UNESCO as a single World Heritage listing. Twenty-five decorated caves. More than a hundred and fifty ancient dwelling sites. And on Monday and Tuesday, you're going to stand inside two of the most important rooms our species has ever made.

So this is episode one of three for the flight. Consider it the deep foundation. Episode two is about the paintings themselves — Lascaux, Font-de-Gaume, and the question of why art exists at all. Episode three jumps forward and does the castles, the wars, and, crucially, the duck fat. But this episode goes down to bedrock, because here's the thing that makes the Dordogne make sense, the single idea to hold onto for the whole week:

Everything you will see — every cave painting, every cliff castle, every honey-colored village, every vineyard — exists because of one rock. Limestone. The entire trip is one long conversation between water and limestone, running for about a hundred million years. Once you see that, the whole region snaps into focus.

Let's start with names, because the region has two and they're both on every sign you'll pass. Dordogne — dor-DON-yuh — is the modern administrative department, named for its biggest river. Périgord — pay-ree-GOR — is the old province, the name from before the French Revolution, and it's the name locals actually feel. It comes from the Petrocorii, the Gaulish tribe who lived there when the Romans arrived — the name means something like "the four clans." When a menu says Périgord, it's making a promise about tradition. And the Périgord comes in four marketing-friendly colors: green in the north, where you'll be for the wedding, all pasture and oak. White in the middle, for the limestone around Périgueux. Purple in the southwest, for the wine country around Bergerac. And black — Périgord Noir, pay-ree-GOR NWAR — in the southeast, supposedly for its dark oak forests, and that's where almost your entire itinerary lives: Sarlat, the river castles, and both painted caves.

Now let's make the limestone.

Wind the clock back roughly a hundred million years, to the Cretaceous. There is no France. There is no Atlantic as you know it — the ocean you're flying over right now is a narrow young rift still unzipping. Most of what will become southwest France is under a warm, shallow, tropical sea, on the shelf of a basin geologists call the Aquitaine Basin. And that sea is full of the tiniest architecture imaginable: coccolithophores — single-celled plankton that armor themselves in microscopic plates of calcium carbonate — plus shellfish, sea urchins, sponges, the whole quiet economy of a sunlit sea.

Here's the part I love. When each of those organisms dies, its little carbonate skeleton snows down to the seafloor. A slow, constant, invisible snowfall of chalk, millimeters per century. Do that for tens of millions of years and you get beds of limestone hundreds of meters thick. So when you put your palm flat on the cliff face at La Roque Saint-Christophe on Monday afternoon — and you should — you are touching the compacted bodies of trillions of creatures that lived and died in a sea that no longer exists, before Tyrannosaurus rex ever evolved. The cliffs of the Dordogne are not just old. They are made of accumulated lives. Emily, if Mike goes quiet and stares at a rock wall at some point this week, this is what he's thinking about. Give him a minute.

Then the sea leaves. Africa begins its long, slow shove into Europe, the collision that's still going on today — it raised the Pyrenees to the south and the Alps to the east, and it gently flexed and lifted the whole Aquitaine platform out of the water. Not dramatically. The Périgord was never crumpled into mountains, which is exactly why it's so good at being what it is: a broad, slightly tilted layer cake of limestone, raised just high enough for rivers to get to work on it.

And now water enters the story, in the two roles it will play all week: sculptor, and chemist.

The sculptor part is easy to picture. Rivers draining off the Massif Central — the old volcanic highlands to the east — flow west across the tilted platform toward the Atlantic. The Dordogne. The Vézère. Over a few million years they saw down through the limestone like a wire through cheese, but slowly, and with wandering hesitation. That's why both rivers meander in those huge lazy loops you'll drive along on Sunday. And every time the land rose a little more, or an ice age dropped the sea level, the rivers cut deeper, leaving their old floodplains stranded as terraces on the valley sides. Result: sheer golden cliffs, sometimes eighty, a hundred, a hundred fifty meters of exposed Cretaceous limestone, standing above a green river. That is the postcard you'll be driving through on Sunday morning. Now you know what it is: a cross-section of a vanished sea, cut by a patient river.

The chemist part is subtler, and it's what makes the caves. Rainwater is not pure water. As it falls, and especially as it soaks through soil, it picks up carbon dioxide and becomes a very weak acid — carbonic acid. Limestone is calcium carbonate, and calcium carbonate dissolves in acid. Slowly. Molecule by molecule. So every drop of rain that lands on the Périgord for tens of millions of years does a tiny bit of quiet chemistry on its way down through the cracks. Cracks widen into fissures. Fissures into conduits. Conduits into galleries. Where the water table sits, whole horizontal cave systems dissolve out of the rock like tunnels bored by a ghost. Geologists call this kind of dissolved, holey, secret-plumbing landscape karst.

And then — this is the beautiful two-step — the rivers cut the valleys deeper, the water table drops with them, and those flooded galleries drain out and are left high and dry in the cliff faces. Empty. Dark. Waiting. A cave like Font-de-Gaume — fon-duh-GOHM — is essentially a fossil drain. The water that made it moved on hundreds of thousands of years ago, and what's left is a dry stone corridor into the hill, at a convenient height above the valley floor, with a stable temperature of about fourteen degrees Celsius year-round. Remember that number when you're shivering in there on Tuesday — you're feeling the average annual temperature of the Périgord, held in stone, because rock that thick doesn't notice seasons. It's the same reason the wine cellars in Saint-Émilion work, by the way. Same rock, same physics, different cargo.

One more piece of geology, because it explains Monday afternoon: the rock shelter. The French word is abri — ah-BREE — and you'll see it on signs all week. The limestone layer cake isn't uniform; some beds are harder, some softer. Where a river once ran against a cliff, or where frost gets into a softer bed — water freezing in the pores, expanding nine percent, popping grains off the face, thousands of winters in a row — the soft layer retreats and the hard layer above it holds, and you get a deep natural overhang. A stone awning, sometimes hundreds of meters long. Not a cave — open to the light and air, but roofed against rain and snow. La Roque Saint-Christophe, which you'll walk on Monday, is a full kilometer of these shelves stacked eighty meters high. Laugerie-Basse, your Tuesday warm-up option, is another. Keep the distinction in mind, because it organizes everything: people lived in the rock shelters. They painted in the caves. Homes in the light. Art in the dark. Hold that thought — it's the door episode two walks through.

Right. The stage is built. Limestone, cliffs, caves, shelters, rivers. Now let's talk about why this particular valley became, for something like twenty thousand years, arguably the best place on the planet to be a human being.

Picture the Vézère valley at the coldest depths of the last Ice Age, around twenty thousand years ago. Northern Europe is under an ice sheet kilometers thick — Scandinavia, most of Britain, gone, white. But the ice never reaches the Périgord. Southwest France sits in a sheltered pocket — far enough south, close enough to the Atlantic's moderating breath, tucked in the lee of the Massif Central. Biologists call places like this refugia: pockets where life holds on while the world around them freezes. It's not warm — think the climate of present-day northern Scandinavia — but it's livable. There are no forests to speak of; it's open steppe-tundra, grasses and dwarf willow and juniper, endless and treeless.

And across that steppe move the animals. This is the part that's genuinely hard to picture from a modern airplane seat, so let me try. The valley you will drive through on Monday — the quiet one with the walnut orchards and the canoe-rental signs — sat on one of the great migration corridors of Ice Age Europe. Reindeer in herds of thousands, moving between winter and summer pastures, funneled by the river valleys, crossing at predictable fords, twice a year, every year, like a tide made of meat and hide and antler. Horses. Bison. Aurochs — wild cattle six feet at the shoulder; remember the name, because they're the stars of Lascaux's ceiling. Ibex on the cliffs. Woolly mammoth and woolly rhinoceros in the cold snaps. Salmon running up the rivers in numbers we'd now call impossible. One French prehistorian described Ice Age Périgord as a cold Serengeti, and honestly that undersells the fish.

Now add the real estate. The south-facing rock shelters — and notice, when you're at La Roque Saint-Christophe, that the great occupied cliffs mostly do face south — are natural solar collectors. The cliff face soaks up low winter sun all day and radiates it back into the shelter through the evening. A shelter with a fire, a hide windbreak, and eighty meters of stone at your back is a warm, dry, defensible home with a commanding view of the river ford where the reindeer cross. Fresh water below. Flint — beautiful, glassy, knappable flint — weathering out of the limestone all around you, some of the best toolmaking stone in Europe, free for the picking.

Food. Water. Fuel. Stone. Shelter. Warmth. Sightlines. The Vézère valley wasn't just survivable, it was rich. And when a place is rich enough that you don't spend every waking hour on calories, interesting things start happening in the evenings. That's not a throwaway line, by the way — that's one of the actual leading theories for why this valley, of all places, exploded with art. Surplus buys time, and time buys culture. But I'm getting ahead of myself; that's episode two.

First, we need to meet the residents. Because here's a number worth sitting with: the Vézère valley has been continuously significant to humans for at least four hundred thousand years. Your week there is a rounding error on the lease.

The earliest tenants we know well are the Neanderthals, and their ancestors before them. And I want to spend a minute rehabilitating them, because the valley you're visiting is one of the places that taught science to stop sneering. At a site called La Micoque — lah mee-KOCK, a few minutes from where you'll park in Les Eyzies — stone tools go back somewhere around three to four hundred thousand years. At Le Moustier — luh moo-STYAY, directly below the cliff of La Roque Saint-Christophe, you'll drive right past it Monday afternoon — archaeologists found such a distinctive Neanderthal toolkit that the entire global culture is named for the hamlet: the Mousterian. When a textbook in Tokyo or Nairobi describes Neanderthal technology, it uses the name of a village you'll pass on your way to dinner. This happens over and over in the Périgord — La Madeleine, La Gravette, La Ferrassie — tiny local places whose names became the chapter headings of human prehistory, because this is where the type sites are. The valley isn't just in the textbook. The valley named the textbook.

And the Neanderthals of these sites were not the grunting cavemen of the old cartoons. They made elegant, efficient stone tools using a technique — preparing a flint core so a single strike pops off a perfect finished flake — that takes modern flint-knappers years to master. They controlled fire, made birch-bark glue, hunted the biggest and most dangerous animals on the steppe at close range with heavy thrusting spears. And at La Ferrassie, just up a side valley from Les Eyzies, they buried their dead — an entire group, adults and children, including a newborn, laid deliberately into pits. Whatever was or wasn't in their heads, something in them refused to leave a body out for the hyenas. On Monday morning, in the museum at Les Eyzies, you will stand in front of the actual skeleton of a Neanderthal — look for the young one from Le Moustier — and I'd invite you to spend a moment just being a fellow person about it.

Then, somewhere around forty-five thousand years ago, new people walk in. Homo sapiens — anatomically, people exactly like you and Emily and everyone else on this plane — arriving in Europe out of Africa by way of the Levant, in small bands, over thousands of years. Science used to call the European members of this wave Cro-Magnons, and here is my favorite piece of local trivia in the entire region: Cro-Magnon is not a scientific term from a lab. It's the name of a specific small rock shelter behind a hotel in Les Eyzies. In 1868, workers building the railway line — the very line that made the town reachable — dug into the shelter and found ancient skeletons with shell ornaments. A geologist named Louis Lartet excavated properly and found individuals including the famous so-called Old Man of Cro-Magnon, whose skull had healed injuries and a face ravaged by infection. He'd survived things that should have killed him, for years — which means people fed him, and kept him, and carried him along. Care is one of the oldest artifacts in the valley. The shelter sits maybe four hundred meters from where you'll be eating pastries from Pâtisserie Margot. Every time any human anywhere is called Cro-Magnon, they are being named after a spot behind a hotel in the village where you're buying lunch.

Now — the overlap. When the sapiens newcomers arrived, Neanderthals were already here, and had been for hundreds of thousands of years. The two kinds of human shared Europe, and plausibly this very valley, for several thousand years. What happened between them is one of the great open questions, but modern genetics has given us one astonishing hard fact: they met, and they had children. If you're of European or Asian descent, roughly one to two percent of your genome is Neanderthal. You are not going to visit the world of some extinct alien species this week. You're going to visit the in-laws. Given that you're headed to a wedding — a merging of two family lines at a château in the Périgord — there's something fitting about honeymooning, so to speak, in the valley where humanity did its most consequential intermarrying.

By around forty thousand years ago, the Neanderthals fade out as a distinct people — climate stress, thin numbers, absorption; the honest answer is we don't fully know — and the valley belongs to sapiens. And over the next thirty thousand years, through the deepest cold, wave after wave of cultures flourish here, each named — of course — for a Périgord site. The Gravettians, named for La Gravette near Bayac, the people of the Venus figurines. The Solutreans, with stone points so thin and symmetrical they ring like porcelain, a craftsmanship far beyond practical need. And finally, from about eighteen to twelve thousand years ago, the Magdalenians — mag-dah-lay-NYAN — named for the La Madeleine shelter on the Vézère, a few bends downstream from Lascaux. The Magdalenians are the high civilization of the Ice Age: needles and sewn clothing, spear-throwers that doubled a hunter's range, harpoons for the salmon runs, jewelry, flutes, trade networks moving seashells hundreds of kilometers inland — and art. Art absolutely everywhere: carved into spear-throwers, engraved on pebbles, and painted, unforgettably, on the walls of the deep caves. Font-de-Gaume is theirs. Most of what you'll see Tuesday is theirs. Lascaux is a little earlier, but it belongs to the same long tradition.

Here's a scale check, because numbers this large go slippery. Lascaux's paintings are very roughly twenty-one thousand years old at the oldest estimates, seventeen at the youngest. The pyramids of Giza are about four and a half thousand years old. So from the painters of Lascaux to the pharaohs is more than three times the distance from the pharaohs to your boarding passes. When people call this stuff ancient history, they're off by an order of category. This is before history had anywhere to stand. And yet — this is the part that will get you in the caves — the people are not remote. They had needles. They had music. They cared for their injured. They held their children up to leave handprints on cave walls. The distance is vast and the humanity is instant, and holding both of those at once is, I think, the real point of your Monday and Tuesday.

One more thing before this episode's plane lands, because Mike, you'll want to know how we know all this. The confidence I've been narrating with — "twenty thousand years ago the valley was steppe-tundra full of reindeer" — sounds like guesswork until you see the receipts, and the receipts are wonderful.

Receipt one: the layer cake. Remember that people lived in the same rock shelters for tens of thousands of years, and every generation dropped things — bones, ash, broken tools, lost beads — onto the floor, and dust and roof-fall slowly buried each layer under the next. A long-occupied shelter like Laugerie-Basse or Le Moustier is therefore a book, read bottom to top: Neanderthal hearths in the deep chapters, Magdalenian needlework near the surface, sterile layers marking the centuries when cold or flood drove everyone out. The men who first dug here in the eighteen-sixties read the book with pickaxes and lost whole pages doing it; Denis Peyrony — pay-ro-NEE, the local schoolteacher who became the father-figure of French prehistory and founded your Monday museum in 1913 — taught the region to read it with trowels and patience instead. Nearly everything I've told you comes, ultimately, out of those layers, most of them within a bicycle ride of Les Eyzies.

Receipt two: the climate archives, and this is the pop-science gem. How do we know what the weather was doing eighteen thousand years ago? Three independent recording devices, all running unattended. Ice cores from Greenland: every year's snowfall traps a sample of that year's air in bubbles — drill three kilometers down and you have an annual archive of the atmosphere going back over a hundred thousand years, readable like tree rings. Pollen: every lake bed and bog downwind of the Périgord has been quietly cataloguing the vegetation for millennia, because each species' pollen grains are distinctive and nearly indestructible — a core of lake mud is a time-lapse film of the landscape flickering from tundra herbs to birch scrub to oak forest as the ice lets go. And the caves themselves: stalagmites grow in annual bands whose chemistry records temperature and rainfall — the limestone was keeping a weather diary the whole time the painters worked. Line all three archives up and they agree, year-band by year-band, and that agreement is why I can tell you with a straight face that the coldest depth of the last Ice Age peaked around twenty-one thousand years ago — right around the older estimates for Lascaux. The bulls may have been painted within sight of the worst weather this valley ever had. Art at the bottom of the cold; make of that what you will.

Receipt three, my favorite because it's so cheeky: the seasons of dead reindeer. Reindeer teeth grow in annual layers like tiny trees, and antlers grow and drop on a strict calendar that differs between males and females. So when archaeologists sift a hearth and find jaws and antler fragments, they can often say not just "they ate reindeer" but "they ate this reindeer in late autumn." Do that across a whole shelter and you get the annual rhythm of a community — here in spring for the salmon run, upriver in autumn for the migration crossings. The Ice Age Périgord had a social calendar, reconstructed one tooth at a time. When you stand in the museum Monday looking at what seems like a dull case of jawbones, that case is a datebook.

Let me land the plane — this episode's plane, anyway — with the shape of your own week, laid over all this deep time. On Sunday you'll drive the Dordogne valley: the river, the cliffs, the castles on the cliffs. That's the limestone wearing its medieval costume. On Monday you cross into the Vézère valley and go straight down the timeline: the museum at Les Eyzies to load the context, then Lascaux to see the masterpiece, then La Roque Saint-Christophe, where the whole story stacks up in one cliff — Neanderthal hearths at the bottom, a medieval fortress town at the top, fifty-five thousand years of tenancy in eighty vertical meters. Tuesday, Font-de-Gaume: the real thing, original paint, the emotional summit of the trip. Then a wedding in the green hills, which — after a week of burials and handprints and family trees forty thousand years deep — will feel less like a break from the theme and more like its conclusion. People finding each other and throwing a feast about it: the oldest continuously running event in the valley.

One last September note before I let you doze. You're arriving in the sweet gap the French call the rentrée — the country has gone back to school and work, the August crowds are gone, but everything is still open and the weather is usually the year's most gracious: warm afternoons, crisp mornings, and — watch for this — river mist. On Monday and Tuesday mornings, the Vézère often lies under a low white quilt of fog that burns off by mid-morning. If you see it from the car, you're seeing the valley exactly as the Magdalenians saw it on ten thousand September mornings: cliffs floating on cloud, river hidden, world gone soft. It photographs beautifully and it costs nothing.

# Episode 2 — The Painted World
*Listen on the plane. Part two of three. Approx. 30 minutes.*

> Production note: the cave art episode — the discovery of Lascaux, how and possibly why the paintings were made, the dating science, the conservation tragedy that explains why Monday is a replica and Tuesday is real. Slow the TTS slightly for this one if your engine allows it.

**SCRIPT**

September the eighth, 1940. Keep the date in your head, because you're going to be in the Périgord almost exactly eighty-six years later, nearly to the day.

France has fallen. The armistice is ten weeks old; the Dordogne sits in the unoccupied zone, full of refugees and rationing and dread. And in the small river town of Montignac — mon-teen-YACK — an eighteen-year-old garage apprentice named Marcel Ravidat — rah-vee-DAH — is out walking on the hill above town with his dog. The dog's name, magnificently, is Robot. Robot goes down a hole. The hole sits where an old pine tree blew down years before, tearing open the ground, and local legend has always said there's a tunnel under the hill — maybe leading to buried treasure of the old manor. Ravidat pulls the dog out, notes the spot, and comes back four days later, September twelfth, with three friends — Jacques Marsal, Georges Agnel, Simon Coencas — a homemade oil lamp, and a knife to widen the hole.

He drops through a fifteen-meter scramble of rubble and finds himself in a large dark chamber. He lifts the lamp. And out of the darkness above his head surge the bulls.

Bulls the size of cars — one of them over five meters long, still the largest single figure ever found in cave art anywhere. Horses at full gallop. Stags with antlers like candelabras. Not scratches, not smudges — vast, confident, finished paintings, oxide-red and charcoal-black and ochre-yellow, wrapped across the white calcite ceiling like weather. Four teenage boys, in the worst month of the worst year of the twentieth century, standing under the most beautiful room left behind by the Ice Age. Marsal said later that the paintings seemed to be moving in the lamplight. Hold onto that detail; it may turn out to be the whole secret, and I'll come back to it.

They told their schoolmaster; the schoolmaster told the one man in France you'd want told. The abbé Henri Breuil — ah-BAY BRUY — a priest and the world's leading authority on prehistoric art, who happened to be sheltering nearby from the war. Breuil arrived within days, looked, and pronounced it genuine and supreme. The nickname he helped hang on it stuck: the Sistine Chapel of Prehistory. Two of those boys, by the way, became the cave's lifelong guardians — Jacques Marsal spent most of the rest of his life as a guide and protector of Lascaux, having essentially never left the hill he fell into at fourteen. And one more turn of the story, since the war never stays out of it: Simon Coencas was Jewish, and survived internment at Drancy by the thinnest of margins before going into hiding. He lived until 2020 — the last of the four, an old man who as a boy had been one of the first modern humans to see the bulls.

So what is Lascaux — lass-KOH? By the final count: about six hundred painted figures and close to six thousand engraved ones, through a couple hundred meters of chambers and passages. Almost entirely animals — horses most numerous, then aurochs, the giant wild cattle, then stags, ibex, bison, a few big cats deep in the back, one bear, one woolly rhinoceros. One strange spotted two-horned creature nobody can identify, nicknamed the unicorn. And exactly one human figure in the entire cave — stick-thin, bird-headed, falling backward in front of a wounded bison, next to a bird on a pole. Down a shaft, in the least accessible spot in the cave. Nobody knows what it means. Everybody who sees it knows it means something. You'll stand in front of it Monday, and I'll say more in the short episode that day.

The dates: somewhere between seventeen and twenty-one thousand years old, on the cusp between the Solutrean and Magdalenian cultures you met in episode one. How do we know? Two main tricks, and Mike, this is for you. First, radiocarbon dating — where the art itself contains charcoal, or where hearths and lamps and pigment-stained tools litter the floor, you can count carbon-fourteen decay like a clock; Lascaux's floor was generously littered. Second, for caves where the art is pure mineral pigment with no carbon to date — and Font-de-Gaume is largely in this camp — you date the thin films of calcite that have dripped over the paintings since they were made. Calcite locks in traces of uranium, uranium decays to thorium on a known schedule, so a calcite skin over a bison gives you a minimum age: the painting must be older than the drip. It's dating by counting atomic grains of sand through the world's slowest hourglass, and it's the technique that produced the biggest shock in the field: the cave of Chauvet, far east of the Dordogne, painted with breathtaking sophistication thirty-six thousand years ago — twice the age of Lascaux, twice as far from Lascaux as Lascaux is from us — and every bit as accomplished. Cave art didn't crawl upward from crude to fine, the way the Victorians assumed. It arrives in the record already brilliant. Which rearranges the question from how did they learn to paint, to something far more interesting: what was painting for?

Before we get to the why, though, let's do the how — because the engineering is half the wonder, and it's the half people miss.

Start with the paint. There are no tubes in the Ice Age. The palette is the earth itself: ochres — iron oxides — for the reds, yellows, and browns; manganese oxides for the blacks; at Lascaux the blacks are mostly manganese rather than charcoal, one reason dating it stays contentious. The painters ground these minerals to powder on stone palettes, mixed them with water, cave clay, sometimes animal fat, and applied them with every technique in the modern kit: fingers, hide pads, brushes of hair or chewed twig, and — most hauntingly — the airbrush. They'd load pigment into a hollow bird bone, or straight into their mouths, and spray a controlled mist. Much of Lascaux is spray-painted. When you see the soft gradient on a horse's belly on Monday, that's someone's breath, held and released twenty thousand years ago, still hanging on the wall.

Now the logistics, because this is where it stops being doodling and becomes a project. Parts of Lascaux's ceiling are five, six meters up. In the painted galleries, archaeologists found sockets cut into the rock walls — mortises for wooden beams. Scaffolding. Someone felled trees, hauled timber into a pitch-black cave, and built work platforms, exactly the way Michelangelo would, seventeen thousand years before Michelangelo. They lit the work with stone lamps burning animal tallow — a beautiful carved red sandstone lamp was found in the shaft, and you'll see lamps like it in the museum Monday morning — plus pine torches. Pigments had to be sourced, some from kilometers away; there's evidence of heating ochre to shift its color. Add it up: raw material supply chains, tool manufacture, lighting technology, carpentry, division of labor, and almost certainly apprenticeship — you don't paint a five-meter aurochs correctly on the first try. This is not a hobby. This is an institution. Whatever the caves were, the community poured its surplus into them the way medieval towns poured surplus into cathedrals — which is why, when Breuil said Sistine Chapel, he was being more precise than he knew.

And the painters knew things about their subjects that took science until basically now to verify. My favorite example: many cave horses, including some at Lascaux, are painted with spotted leopard-like coats, and for a century scholars said, obviously symbolic, horses don't come like that. Then in 2011 geneticists pulled DNA from Ice Age horse bones and found the leopard-spotting gene. The horses did come like that. The painters were right, and the professors were wrong, and I find that outcome deeply satisfying. Watch for it Monday: these are not generic animals, they're portraits of a specific fauna — winter coats on the horses, the beard on the aurochs, antlers in autumn condition. Which, incidentally, is one of the ways we know a lot of this art engages with seasons — some scholars read parts of Lascaux as organized around the migration year: horses in spring condition here, stags in autumn rut there. You're visiting in September. On the Ice Age calendar painted on those walls, that's stag season and salmon season. You're arriving on time.

So. Why? Why crawl hundreds of meters into the dark to paint animals you could see out the front door of your rock shelter?

Honest answer up front: nobody knows, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling a book. But the theories are a pleasure in themselves, so let me give you the tour — five doors, none of them fully open, none of them fully closed.

Door one, the oldest: art for art's sake. They painted because it's glorious and humans like glorious things. Almost certainly true as far as it goes — but it doesn't explain the location. You don't hang your art where no one can see it, in places that take ropes and lamps and crawling to reach.

Door two, Breuil's own: hunting magic. Paint the beast to capture the beast; some animals do seem marked with wounds or projectiles. But there's a famous inconvenient fact, and you'll be living it all week: the painters of the Vézère ate reindeer, overwhelmingly — the bone piles outside the shelters are wall-to-wall reindeer. The walls, though, are horses and bison and aurochs. Almost no reindeer. At Lascaux, effectively none. They did not paint their dinner. Whatever the animals on the walls are, they aren't a menu.

Door three, the structuralist: the caves are organized cosmology — certain species paired with certain species, certain animals assigned to entrances, depths, and shafts. Statistically, the patterns are real; horses and bison do keep specific company across hundreds of caves. What the grammar says, we can't read. But the fact that there is a grammar tells you this was a system, not decoration.

Door four, the one with the most modern momentum: the shamanic theory. The deep cave as the membrane between worlds; the paintings made in — or about — trance states, the animals as spirit-beings emerging from the rock. Its evidence includes the way so many figures use the natural rock — a bulge becoming a shoulder, a crack becoming a horn, as if the animal were already in the wall and merely traced — and those abstract dot-and-grid signs that recur across caves and match the geometric hallucinations all human nervous systems produce in early trance. It's seductive, it fits a lot, and it's unfalsifiable, which makes scientists itchy. Take it as a strong maybe.

And door five, the newest wing: information. Researchers have catalogued the abstract signs — dots, strokes, quadrilaterals — and found a small stable vocabulary repeating across all of Ice Age Europe for twenty thousand years. One recent, hotly debated proposal reads dot-and-line sequences next to animals as a phenological calendar — roughly, notes on how many lunar months after spring the species mates or migrates or births. If even a version of that holds up, then the caves are, among other things, humanity's first databases, and writing has a twenty-thousand-year prologue scratched next to a horse in the Dordogne. The jury is very much out. But standing in front of a panel wondering whether you're looking at a prayer or a spreadsheet — that's a good problem to have on a Monday.

And one more thing that isn't a theory, just a fact I want you to carry in: the children. Cave floors and walls across the region preserve children's footprints and small handprints, sometimes high up, where an adult must have lifted them. Whatever happened in the caves, children were brought along, held up, included. When the guide's flashlight finds a handprint Tuesday, remember it's as likely to be a nine-year-old's as a shaman's — and that somebody's parent was doing the lifting. Twenty thousand years before your wedding week, and the family unit is right there on the wall.

Two more rooms of this world before the tragedy, because the paintings are only the loudest part of the art.

First: the engravings. Here's a number that surprises people — at Lascaux, the engraved figures vastly outnumber the painted ones, and whole caves nearby, like Combarelles, are almost entirely engraved: hundreds upon hundreds of figures scratched into the rock with flint, some bold as woodcuts, some so fine you need raking light to catch them. Engraving may actually be the workhorse medium of the Ice Age, with paint as the gala format. Keep that in mind at both caves: whenever the light rakes sideways, look for the scratch-lines riding under and over the pigment. Often the painting sits on an engraved sketch — underdrawing, exactly as an art teacher would demand — and sometimes the engraving corrects the paint. Somebody, seventeen thousand years ago, looked at a nearly finished horse and thought: the jaw is wrong. And fixed it. That tiny act of revision might be the most relatable thing in either cave.

Second: the hands. All over Ice Age Europe — less at Lascaux, famously at other caves — people left hand stencils: palm to the wall, pigment sprayed over, a negative print that reads across the millennia as unmistakably as a wave. And the hands have been talking to science lately. Measure the finger-length ratios and many of those hands are plausibly female, which quietly rearranged a century of assumptions about who made this art; the old picture of the lone male shaman-artist now has to share the scaffold. Other prints are children's, held up by someone taller. Some hands show missing finger segments — frostbite, ritual, or simply fingers folded down as a signal code; nobody knows. The one thing a hand stencil can't be is anonymous-in-general: each one was a specific person, on a specific cold day, saying the oldest sentence there is — I was here.

And third, briefly: the sound. The Ice Age had music — actual instruments survive, bone flutes elsewhere in Europe with true finger holes, and from this wider world, whistles and bullroarers. And researchers who've mapped cave acoustics report something suggestive: in several caves, panels of art cluster near points of strong resonance — where a voice or a drum booms most richly. It's contested, like everything in this field. But stand in the Hall of the Bulls on Monday and try to imagine the room as it may actually have run: firelight moving, animals flickering in and out of the dark, and sound — chant, drum, drone — rolling off the calcite. A painted cave may be the ancestor not of the museum but of the opera house. Then the house lights come up, so to speak, and we reach the part of the story where the twentieth century breaks the spell.

Now for the tragedy — because you need it to understand why your Monday ticket says Lascaux four, not Lascaux.

After the war, the cave was fitted with a door, stairs, lighting, and opened to the public in 1948. It became one of the wonders of the world overnight — up to a couple thousand visitors a day at the peak, more than a million people over fifteen years. Picasso reputedly came out saying, in effect, we have invented nothing. And every single visitor exhaled.

Here's the pop-science core of it, and it's painfully elegant. A sealed cave is a stable system: constant fourteen-to-sixteen degrees, saturated humidity, almost no airflow. The paintings had survived seventeen thousand years because nothing changed. Then suddenly: a thousand humans a day, each one a walking furnace radiating a hundred watts, each exhaling warm, damp carbon dioxide by the lungful, each shedding skin flakes and pollen and spores. The carbon dioxide plus moisture formed exactly what episode one taught you to fear — carbonic acid, condensing on the walls, the same chemistry that carved the caves now dissolving the paint. Body heat set up air currents the cave had never known. Green algae began creeping across the walls — la maladie verte, the green sickness. Then a white crust of calcite began to grow over paintings. The cave that had preserved humanity's masterpiece for seventeen millennia was being destroyed in fifteen years by admiration.

In 1963, André Malraux — France's culture minister; remember the name, he saves Sarlat with a different law, that's episode three — shut Lascaux. Permanently. The decision was agonizing and correct, and the crises kept coming anyway: a fungal invasion in 2001, black mold spots in the two thousands, each fought back by a permanent scientific council that now runs the cave like an intensive-care patient — sealed, climate-monitored, entered only rarely, by a handful of people, for minutes at a time. You and I will never see the real Lascaux. That door has closed for our species' foreseeable future, and it closed because a million of us loved it wrong.

But here's where the story turns back toward you two, because the French response to losing the cave was gloriously French: they decided to remake it. Perfectly. Twice. Lascaux two, opened in 1983 beside the original hill, reproduced the two great galleries and was for decades one of the most-visited replicas on earth. And then Lascaux four — your Monday, opened in 2016 at the foot of the hill — is the full-scale endgame: virtually the entire decorated cave, rebuilt from laser scans accurate to fractions of a millimeter, the art repainted by teams of artists over years, using the original palette of pigments, on sculpted panels that reproduce every bump and crack of the rock — because remember, the painters used the bumps and cracks, so a flat copy would be a lie. The temperature is held cave-cool at sixteen degrees, the light is kept torch-dim, on purpose. It is, by broad consent, the best replica of anything, anywhere.

I know the word replica itches. Let it go, and think of it the way you'd think of hearing a great orchestra play a symphony whose manuscript is locked in a vault: the performance is real even when the paper stays home. And your specific format — the self-guided visit with audioguide, just the two of you setting the pace — is honestly the best way this site can be seen now. The historical complaints about Lascaux four were rushed groups and fixed pacing. You've accidentally bought the version with neither. Stand under the bulls as long as you like. That's a luxury the cave itself never offered anyone, including the Magdalenians, who worked by guttering lamplight in cramped air.

Which brings us to Tuesday, and I'll keep this short because it gets its own episode on the walk-up. Font-de-Gaume, in the hill right above Les Eyzies, is the other side of the bargain: the last cave in France with polychrome paintings — the full multi-color, Lascaux-class art — that you can still walk into and see original. Not a copy, not a projection: the actual pigment, laid on by actual hands, fourteen thousand-ish years ago, with the actual darkness of the actual hill pressing in around you. About two hundred figures, bison above all — a famous frieze of five — and one image, a reindeer tenderly licking the forehead of another, that has made more archaeologists cry than they'd admit in print. Numbers are capped at a few dozen visitors a day, cut year by year for conservation, and the honest expectation is that someday, maybe soon, Font-de-Gaume closes like Lascaux closed. You two have tickets for eleven fifteen on a Tuesday in September while the door is still open. Your grandchildren may know these paintings the way you'll know Lascaux — through a magnificent copy. You'll be able to tell them you stood in the room.

And that's the real design of your week, whether or not you planned it this way: Monday is comprehension — the museum gives you the language, the replica gives you the whole text, bright-lit in your control. Tuesday is presence — harder to see, dimmer, briefer, guided, and absolutely irreplaceable. Clarity, then contact. It's the right order, and almost nobody sequences it as well as you two are about to.

Last thought before you stretch your legs. Remember Marsal saying the paintings seemed to move in the lamplight? Researchers have taken that seriously in recent years — because under a flickering flame, with the eye's persistence, many of these figures are built to move. Multiple superimposed legs on the horses. Sequential heads. The animals painted across curved surfaces so a walking viewer sees them stride. There's a respectable argument that what you'll see Monday isn't just humanity's first painting. It might be humanity's first animation — designed for a moving light source and a moving audience, twenty thousand years before anyone said cinema. Four boys and a dog fell through the ceiling of the world's oldest movie theater, in 1940, while the world burned. And the projector, so to speak, still runs.

# Episode 3 — Duck Fat and Gunpowder
*Listen on the plane. Part three of three. Approx. 30 minutes.*

> Production note: the medieval-history and food episode. Covers the Eleanor of Aquitaine backstory, the Hundred Years' War as it plays out on Sunday's castles, the bastides, and then the full food-and-terroir briefing including September seasonality and market etiquette.

**SCRIPT**

Welcome back. Last episode ended in the dark, fourteen thousand years ago, with a reindeer licking another reindeer's forehead. This episode ends with dinner. In between: about a thousand years of people fighting over the exact stretch of riverbank where you two will be eating lunch on Sunday, and the story of how a landscape at war became a landscape of walnut groves and duck fat and the best market towns in France. The connective tissue, as ever, is the limestone. The cliffs that sheltered the Magdalenians turn out to be equally good at holding castles, and the caves that held paintings turn out to be equally good at aging cheese and hiding Protestants. Nothing in the Périgord is ever used only once.

Fast-forward through the ice melting. Forests return, the reindeer follow the cold north forever, and the hunter-artists' descendants — plus new arrivals — learn farming. The valley never empties; it just keeps switching costumes. The Gauls of the Petrocorii tribe give the region its name and put up a good fight against Julius Caesar. Rome builds a city at Périgueux with an amphitheater and a great circular temple whose tower still stands. Christianity arrives, abbeys sprout — Sarlat itself begins as a Benedictine abbey around the ninth century, allegedly holding relics of Saint Sacerdos. Vikings actually raid up the Dordogne and Vézère rivers in the ninth century — longships this far inland, four hundred kilometers from the sea, which tells you something about both Viking nerve and how navigable your Sunday river really is. People shelter, once again, in the cliffs; the fortress at La Roque Saint-Christophe gets its first serious walls precisely to watch for dragon-prowed ships coming round the bend.

But the story that carves your Sunday itinerary into shape begins with a wedding. Fitting, given why you're flying over.

In 1137, the Duke of Aquitaine dies on pilgrimage, leaving everything to his daughter Eleanor — Aliénor in the local tongue, ah-lyay-NOR — who at fifteen is suddenly the greatest heiress in Europe. Aquitaine — ah-kee-TEN — is not a province; it's a third of France, including every inch of the Dordogne. She's married within months to the King of France, Louis the Seventh. It goes badly. He's monkish; she's brilliant, political, worldly — she rides on Crusade with him and comes back openly unimpressed. After fifteen years and no sons, the marriage is annulled in March of 1152.

Eight weeks later — eight weeks — Eleanor marries Henry Plantagenet, a nineteen-year-old with red hair, boundless energy, and a claim on the English throne. Two years after that, he's crowned King Henry the Second of England. And here is the sentence that detonates three centuries of history: Eleanor's dowry goes with her. The King of England now rules more of France than the King of France — including the entire Dordogne. From that wedding until 1453 — three hundred years — the southwest is the fault line where the two crowns grind against each other, and your Sunday river is, for long stretches, the actual border. That's the sound you'll be hearing under everything on Sunday afternoon: one bad marriage and one brilliant remarriage, echoing off the cliffs.

The grinding starts immediately — Eleanor and Henry's own sons rebel, and their most famous son, Richard the Lionheart, spends years stomping around the Périgord subduing local barons, including, for a time, taking Beynac — bay-NACK — the castle you'll storm at four fifteen on Sunday. But the main event is the Hundred Years' War, 1337 to 1453, when the simmer becomes open dynastic war. And in the Dordogne it's not the war of the textbooks, all Agincourt and Joan of Arc. Here it's a war of castles, raids, and protection rackets — a cold war with hot edges, fought valley by valley for four generations.

Sunday afternoon, you get its perfect stage set. Château de Beynac, on the north bank, glowering French. And directly across the river — you'll see it plainly from the ramparts and even better from the gardens at Marqueyssac — the Château de Castelnaud, kass-tel-NOH, which spent most of the war in English hands. Two fortresses, in sight of each other, close enough that on a still day each could hear the other's bells, held by opposite sides in a century of war. Between them, the river, the fords, the tolls, and the peasants trying to get a harvest in. The castles traded raids, spies, and the occasional sortie for decades. Standing on Beynac's ramparts looking across at Castelnaud is the single best cold-war viewpoint of the middle ages anywhere in Europe, and you'll have it in late-afternoon light.

While you're up there, notice what a medieval castle actually is, because Beynac is an unusually honest one — never prettified into a Renaissance palace. It's a machine, and the machine's product is control of movement. The keep is twelfth-century, walls up to a couple meters thick, on a cliff a hundred and fifty meters above the river. From that height you see everything that moves on the water and the valley road, and everything that moves pays. River tolls, road tolls, market rights, mill rights — a castle is a tollbooth with a sword. That's why they cluster along this river like beads: the Dordogne was the highway, and the highway was worth taxing. And notice the human scale inside — the rooms are small, dark, cold. Even for the lords of one of the four great baronies of Périgord, which Beynac was, medieval luxury was mostly the luxury of being the one doing the taxing.

Now, the war's supporting cast, because you'll be sleeping inside two pieces of it. First: the bastides — bass-TEED. In the decades before the war, both crowns went on a town-founding spree across the southwest — hundreds of brand-new planned towns, laid out at a stroke on a grid around a central arcaded market square. Militarily they staked territory; economically they were startup incubators: settlers got a plot, tax breaks, and a charter of liberties, in exchange for showing up and making the place real. The French king Philip the Third — or his brother, sources argue — founded Domme, DOM, in 1281 as a French royal bastide on a cliff four hundred feet over the river, staring across at what would become English country. You'll be there Sunday at sunset-ish. Look at the street plan when you walk in: it's a grid, or at least a grid negotiating with a clifftop — the surveyor's straight lines bending where the rock says no. It's the thirteenth century's idea of a master-planned community, and it still works: the market square still holds a market, seven hundred forty years later.

Domme also hides my favorite dark footnote of the region. In 1307, when King Philip the Fair destroyed the Knights Templar in a single coordinated dawn raid — Friday the thirteenth of October, the origin of the superstition, though scholars roll their eyes at the connection — dozens of Templars were imprisoned in Domme's gate towers for years awaiting trial. They carved graffiti into the walls: crosses, Grails, crucifixions, geometric prayers, still visible today scratched into the stone of the Porte des Tours. Losers of history, writing on the wall of history, in a town built as a border weapon. The Périgord keeps everything.

Second piece of supporting cast: Sarlat — sar-LAH — your Sunday night and Monday morning. Sarlat was the abbey town that became the administrative heart of the French Périgord, and it earned its stone the hard way: besieged, raided, ransomed, and rebuilt across both the Hundred Years' War and, later, the Wars of Religion, when Protestant and Catholic armies took turns at it in the fifteen hundreds — the same wars, incidentally, that finally destroyed the cliff city at La Roque Saint-Christophe in 1588, ending fifty-five thousand years of continuous occupancy on one order from one king. When the wars finally ended, Sarlat rebuilt in golden limestone in the style of its best decades, and then — this is the miracle — history simply forgot it. Trade routes moved, the railway mostly passed by, and the town was too poor to modernize, which is the best preservative there is. More on the second miracle, the 1962 law that saved it, in Monday morning's episode — it involves Malraux again, the same man who closed Lascaux. One career, two acts of salvage.

And Sarlat gives you a resident worth meeting over dinner: Étienne de La Boétie — ay-TYEN duh lah bo-ay-SEE — born there in 1530, in a gorgeous ornamented house you'll walk past Monday morning, fifty meters from your hotel. At about eighteen he wrote the Discourse on Voluntary Servitude, one of the founding documents of political philosophy — the argument that tyrants hold power only because people consent to give it, and that the remedy is not rebellion but simple, coordinated refusal. Every civil-disobedience movement since — Thoreau, Tolstoy, Gandhi — stands downstream of a teenager from Sarlat. He died at thirty-two, and his best friend sat by the deathbed and was so marked by the loss that he spent the rest of his life writing about how to live and die well. The friend was Michel de Montaigne, the essays are the Essays, and when Montaigne tried to explain the friendship, he produced the most famous sentence about friendship in the French language: because it was he, because it was I. That's the town you'll have a duck dinner in on Sunday. Try to walk past the La Boétie house twice — evening floodlight and morning sun.

Before the cannons end it, one more figure from the war years, and I'm giving him to you because your itinerary literally drives through his front yard: Bertran de Born — bair-TRAHN duh BORN — lord of Hautefort, oat-FOR, the hilltop village where you're aiming to have dinner on Monday night. In the late twelfth century, Bertran was one of the most celebrated troubadours in Europe — the poet-knights of the south who more or less invented the love song — except Bertran's specialty wasn't love. It was war. He wrote gorgeous, appalling verses about how spring is sweet because campaigning season is starting, and how the sight of besieged castles gladdens the heart — the twelfth century's greatest war correspondent, embedded on his own side. He feuded with his own brother over Hautefort itself, egged on the sons of Eleanor and Henry in their rebellions — Richard the Lionheart was alternately his enemy and his patron — and generally treated the Plantagenet family drama as both raw material and personal chessboard. Dante was so scandalized, a century later, that he placed Bertran deep in the Inferno carrying his own severed head like a lantern — punished as a sower of discord between kin. All of which is to say: when you drive up toward that castle silhouette above Hautefort on Monday evening for a quiet three-course dinner, you're dining in the shadow of the man who taught Europe to sing about exactly the wars whose castles you toured on Sunday. Raise a glass to him; from a safe distance of eight centuries, he's excellent company.

And one register-check on all this castle romance, because it's easy to hear a century of war as adventure: for the people who grew the food, the Hundred Years' War in the Périgord was mostly the routiers — roo-TYAY — freelance mercenary companies who, between official campaigns, simply didn't stop. They occupied castles, taxed valleys at swordpoint, and sold protection against themselves. Villages fortified their churches — you'll see church towers all week with arrow slits, which should strike you as theologically odd until you remember what they were for. Some communities dug refuge tunnels under their houses; some went back into the cliffs, into the same shelters as their ancestors of the ice. The region's population may have fallen by half across the fourteenth century, between war, plague, and the hunger that follows both. The golden villages you're driving through are survivors' architecture: built, burned, rebuilt, and finally — after 1453 — allowed to just be farms again. The prettiness is earned.

So how does the war end? Gunpowder — and it ends practically in your Thursday itinerary. By the fourteen-forties, France has done something new: a standing professional artillery corps, the Bureau brothers' cannon train, the best in the world. Castles like Beynac and Castelnaud — designed against ladders, arrows, and patience — are suddenly obsolete; stone that shrugged off catapults cracks under iron cannonballs. In July 1453, the last English field army in the southwest, under the seventy-ish-year-old veteran John Talbot, charges a fortified French artillery camp at Castillon, on the Dordogne river — about ten kilometers from Saint-Émilion, where you'll be sipping wine on Thursday afternoon. The guns shred the charge; Talbot is killed under his horse. Castillon is the last battle of the Hundred Years' War — the war ends where your trip ends, and the town is still called Castillon-la-Bataille. Bordeaux surrenders three months later, and three hundred years of English Aquitaine are over. When you raise a glass in Saint-Émilion, you'll be doing it on the doorstep of the battlefield that decided whose language the waiter would greet you in.

Right. The gunpowder half is done. Now the duck fat, because the same geography that made the war made the food, and your itinerary is honestly one of the great eating routes of Europe.

Here's the frame: Périgord cuisine is preservation cuisine. It's what a landlocked, hilly, hot-summer, cold-winter region invents when it has abundance in October and needs to eat until April, in the centuries before refrigeration. Every icon on the menu is a solved physics problem.

Exhibit A: confit — kon-FEE. You take duck or goose legs, salt them for a day — salt pulls water out of the meat by osmosis, and microbes need water — then cook them slowly, submerged in their own rendered fat, and pot them up still under that fat. The fat sets into an airtight, waterproof seal; no air, no water, no microbes, no spoilage. A crock of confit in a cool Périgord cellar keeps for months and improves — enzymes keep gently tenderizing the meat all winter. It is canning, invented before cans, and it's why duck fat, not butter or olive oil, is the cooking fat of the region. Which brings us to the greatest side dish in France: pommes sarladaises — pom sar-lah-DEZ, literally potatoes in the style of Sarlat — potatoes crisped in duck fat with garlic and parsley. Duck fat's mix of fats stays liquid enough to crisp beautifully at high heat; science struggles to improve on it. You will be offered these approximately daily. Accept approximately daily; you'll walk it off on the castle stairs.

Exhibit B, and let's be grown-ups about it: foie gras — fwah-GRAH, the fattened liver of duck or goose, the region's flagship and its controversy. The technique of fattening waterfowl is ancient — the Egyptians noticed geese gorge naturally before migration, storing energy in the liver, and the practice runs through Rome to here, where corn's arrival from the New World made the Périgord its world capital. Modern production intensifies that natural gorging by hand-feeding, and you should know that welfare objections to it are serious and I won't argue them away. I'll only give you the local context: in the Périgord this is overwhelmingly small-farm culture, geese and ducks in actual meadows, and the farmer at a market stall will happily talk husbandry with you, and the semi-cooked mi-cuit on a slice of bread with a glass of sweet Monbazillac is, purely as chemistry and pleasure, extraordinary. Decide as you see fit; you'll eat superbly either way, because duck in all its unarguable forms — the confit, and the seared rosy breast called magret, mah-GRAY, a preparation only invented in the nineteen-sixties — carries the menu regardless.

Exhibit C: the walnut — la noix, nwah. This is the tree crop of the Périgord, with its own appellation like a fine wine, and it's everywhere in your week: walnut groves line the Vézère road, walnut bread at the markets, walnut oil in the salads, walnuts in the cake, and vin de noix — van-duh-NWAH — an aperitif of green walnuts steeped in wine and eau-de-vie that you should order once at Essendiéras just to say you did. Walnut oil was medieval liquid gold — it lit lamps, paid tithes, and moved downriver on the flat-bottomed gabarre boats you'll see at La Roque-Gageac. And your timing is sharp: the harvest starts in late September. The nets are going out under the trees the week you're there.

Exhibit D, the one requiring expectation management: the truffle. Yes, the Périgord gave its name to Tuber melanosporum, the black winter truffle; yes, it's a wonder — a subterranean fungus married to oak roots, trading sugars for minerals in the dark, announcing its ripeness by synthesizing a molecule so close to a pig pheromone that sows go berserk for it, which is why tradition used pigs and sense used dogs, who unlike pigs don't try to eat the merchandise. But note the word winter. Truffle season is December to February. A market stall selling "truffle products" in September is selling you last winter in a jar, at tourist prices. Skip it without regret — because September hands you something better, exhibit E: the cèpe, SEP — the porcini of the Périgord. If rain has come in the fortnight before your visit, mid-September is peak cèpe, whole market tables of fat brown boletes, and every blackboard menu in Sarlat will say omelette aux cèpes. That is the correct answer to at least one dinner decision this week. Locals guard their gathering spots like bank codes; asking a Périgourdin where he found his cèpes is charmingly close to asking his salary.

And to drink: you're bracketed by two wine worlds. Thursday you get the aristocrat, Saint-Émilion — full briefing in its own episode, including why the harvest may be happening around you as you drive in. But give the local underdog its due some evening: Bergerac, the Dordogne's own vineyard, unfairly overshadowed by grand Bordeaux next door — same grapes, gentler prices — and above all golden Monbazillac, mon-bah-zee-YACK, a sweet wine made from grapes deliberately left to be attacked by a fungus, Botrytis, the noble rot, which shrivels them into candied intensity on the vine. Fungus-concentrated sunshine with foie gras is the region's oldest power couple. When a waiter suggests it, that's not an upsell, that's heritage.

Last piece, and it doubles as your Sunday-morning briefing: the market. Your very first stop in the region — before any castle, any cave — is the Sunday market at Saint-Cyprien, san-see-pree-AN, and that's the right order of operations, because the weekly market is the region's true institution, older than most of its castles; it's where the bastide squares earn their keep and where the whole food story above is lying on tables. Three rules of French market etiquette, worth rehearsing on the plane. One: say bonjour — bon-ZHOOR — to the stallholder before anything else; in France the greeting opens the transaction, and skipping it is genuinely rude rather than merely brisk. Two: don't handle the produce — point, and the seller picks for you; it's their craft and their stock. Three: carry some cash and unhurried manners — taste what's offered, buy small and often, and say merci beaucoup, mair-SEE boh-KOO, on the way out. Do those three things and the Périgord will treat you like family. Which, per episode one and a certain percentage of your genome, you technically are.

So there's your triptych complete. A hundred million years of limestone; twenty thousand years of painters; a thousand years of castles, and confit as the physics of surviving winter. Tomorrow you'll be on a train south doing three hundred kilometers an hour across all of it, and that ride has its own episode — the backbone tape, the long one. For now: the cabin lights are probably down, you land in the morning, and somewhere south of Paris a valley that has been hosting humans for four hundred thousand years has set two more places at the table. Sleep well. It'll keep. It's good at that.

# Episode 4 — The Backbone: Paris to Bordeaux at Three Hundred Kilometers an Hour
*Listen on the TGV, Saturday September 12. Approx. 45 minutes — about a third of the ride. Sit on the left side of the train heading south if you can; most of the landmarks below fall that way, and you get the afternoon light.*

> Production note: the audio tour for the Montparnasse → Bordeaux Saint-Jean TGV. Structured to run in rough sync with the journey: station, departure, then geography in order — Beauce, Loire, Poitou, Charente, Gironde — then a Bordeaux landing briefing keyed to their Saturday night and 07:00 Sunday car pickup. It is deliberately expansive: engineering, geology, history, wine science.

**SCRIPT**

Mike, Emily — if everything has gone to plan, you are sitting in a train at Gare Montparnasse — gar mon-par-NAHSS — with your bags overhead, a wedding outfit somewhere in the luggage you are trying not to think about, and a departure board that says Bordeaux Saint-Jean. In roughly two hours and a bit, you will step off this train five hundred eighty kilometers to the southwest, having crossed half of France — several major rivers, three ancient provinces, two climate zones, and about a hundred and fifty million years of geology — at speeds touching three hundred twenty kilometers an hour, which is to say, roughly two hundred miles per hour, in a seat, with a coffee. This episode is the story of the machine you're sitting in, the country sliding past your window, and the city waiting at the end. It's the backbone tape: everything this week hangs off the line you're about to travel.

But before the train moves — the station. Montparnasse deserves two stories.

Story one, because no railway episode can skip it: this is the station of the most famous train accident photograph ever taken. October 1895 — the old station building, not this one — an express from Granville comes in late and fast, the driver trying to make up minutes. The air brake fails, the locomotive plows through the buffers, across the concourse, through a meter-thick stone facade, and nose-dives out of the building onto the street below, where it stands for days, vertical, absurd, while all of Paris comes to look. One woman on the street was killed — a newspaper seller; the railway pensioned her children — but every passenger survived, and the photograph of a steam engine planted face-first on a Paris sidewalk became one of the most reproduced images of the industrial age. You've seen it on posters and album covers. It happened here, roughly where you bought your croissant.

Story two is quieter and better. August 1944, liberation of Paris: the German military governor, von Choltitz — who had, at least by the polished version, declined Hitler's order to burn the city — signed his surrender inside this station. Montparnasse is where the occupation of Paris formally ended. Stations collect history because everything passes through them; this one collected the end of a war and the invention of the disaster photo, and now it collects you two, heading to a wedding.

And Montparnasse has always been Paris's door to the southwest — the Breton station, the Atlantic station. Which is why, when France decided to point its high-speed revolution west and south, the new line grew from here.

Now — the machine. Because Mike, I know you'll spend at least ten minutes of this ride wondering what it is doing under you, so let's do the engineering properly.

The TGV — train à grande vitesse, simply "high-speed train" — is one of the great unsung French masterpieces, up there with the caves and the confit. The first line opened in 1981, Paris to Lyon, and the concept has three pillars, each of which you'll feel in the next two hours.

Pillar one: it's not really about the train. It's about the track. A dedicated high-speed line — an LGV, ligne à grande vitesse — is a purpose-built road with no level crossings, no freight, no sharp curves, and no towns to thread. At three hundred kilometers an hour you cover eighty-three meters every second, so a curve you'd barely notice on a country railway would put your coffee in your lap; LGV curves are drawn with radii of six, seven kilometers, and gentle bankings, so the line rides the landscape like a drawn breath — long cuttings, long embankments, long viaducts, never a jink. The rail itself is continuously welded — no clickety-clack, because there are no joints to click; that eerie smoothness you're feeling is the absence of a sound humans associated with trains for a hundred fifty years. And the trainset is articulated: the cars share bogies — the wheel assemblies sit between carriages, not under each one — which makes the train a single stiff spine rather than a chain of wagons. That's pillar two: it's why the ride feels less like a train and more like a very low-flying aircraft, and it has a grim virtue too — in derailment tests and a few real incidents, articulated TGVs have stayed upright and in line at extraordinary speeds. Pillar three is the power: twenty-five thousand volts coming off that overhead wire into motors that would embarrass a small power station, in a train that still keeps its axle weight to about seventeen tonnes so it doesn't hammer the track it depends on. Light, stiff, powered like fury, on a road with no surprises.

How fast can the concept go? In April 2007, a specially tuned TGV with oversized wheels and a shortened set ran five hundred seventy-four point eight kilometers an hour on the eastern line — about three hundred fifty-seven miles per hour, the world record for conventional rail, a record that still stands. That's not your speed today; commercially the fleet cruises at three hundred to three hundred twenty. But it tells you the margin the engineers are holding in reserve. You are traveling at a comfortable sixty percent of what this design has proven it can do.

How France got here is a story worth a minute, because it explains something real about the country you're crossing. France has a two-century-old cult of the state engineer — the great schools, the corps of bridges and roads, the conviction that infrastructure is a form of national literature. In 1955, on a dead-straight stretch of line south of Bordeaux — not far, incidentally, from where you'll land today — two French electric locomotives were run, on separate days, to three hundred thirty-one kilometers an hour. The tests were half triumph, half warning: the speed was real, but the locomotives hunted and swayed viciously and the overhead wire was left hanging in shreds behind them. The lesson France took was the right one — the trains could be made fast, but the whole system would have to be reinvented around the speed: track, wire, signaling, everything. It took twenty-five years, a detour through a gas-turbine prototype that the 1973 oil shock mercifully killed, and a bet-the-railway decision to electrify and build Paris–Lyon as a clean-sheet line. When the TGV opened in 1981 it wasn't a faster train; it was a different transport category wearing a train costume. Most countries never make that leap — they bolt speed onto old railroads and get old-railroad results. France rebuilt the premise. You're sitting in the conclusion.

And your particular stretch of track is the newest big piece of the network: the LGV Sud Europe Atlantique, three hundred-odd kilometers from Tours to Bordeaux, opened in July 2017 — one of the largest infrastructure projects in modern Europe, around eight billion euros, delivered through a giant public-private concession. Before it, Paris to Bordeaux took three hours on a good day. Now the fastest trains do it in about two hours and four minutes, and the consequence was immediate and dramatic: the plane lost. On routes under about three hours, high-speed rail simply beats flying — city center to city center, no security theater, no taxiing — and the Paris-Bordeaux air route withered almost overnight. France later banned short-haul flights where a rail alternative under two and a half hours exists, a law this line made thinkable. You are, right now, aboard the argument.

One more number, because it's the kind you two can trade over dinner this week: per seat, this train emits on the order of one-twentieth to one-fiftieth the carbon of the equivalent flight, because it runs on French electricity, which is mostly nuclear and hydro. The greenest fast machine in Europe, powered largely by atoms, carrying you to look at paintings made by firelight. There's a whole history of human energy in that sentence.

Alright. The train is moving, or about to. Watch the window; here's what France is about to show you, in order.

First, the escape from Paris — fifteen-ish minutes of suburbs, graffiti, and commuter platforms while the train ambles on ordinary track. Somewhere out there to your right is Versailles. Feel free to ignore all of it; the show starts when the train reaches the high-speed line and the driver, so to speak, opens the tap. You'll know the moment. The acceleration is quiet but relentless — no engine roar, just the world's gears changing — and within a few minutes the landscape stops being scenery and starts being flow.

What's flowing past first is the Beauce — the great grain plain southwest of Paris, pool-table flat, horizon to horizon of wheat stubble by the time you see it in September. This is France's breadbasket and has been since the middle ages — the plain that fed Paris, dotted with grain silos standing where you'd expect castles. The flatness is geological: you're crossing the floor of the Paris Basin, a stack of sediments laid in yet another vanished sea. France, you'll find, is mostly vanished seas. If it's late afternoon, the light on the stubble does something Dutch painters would have killed for. Then, about an hour in, the land begins to gather itself into a wide green trench, and you cross the Loire.

The Loire — LWAR — is France's longest river, its last great wild one, all sandbanks and braided channels, and the trench it runs in is the Loire Valley: château country, the so-called garden of France, where the French Renaissance happened — Chambord, Amboise, and the rest strung along the water east and west of your crossing. Leonardo da Vinci died in this valley, at Amboise, upstream to your left, having been imported by King Francis the First as the world's first celebrity acquisition, allegedly arriving with the Mona Lisa in his saddlebags — which is roughly why she lives in Paris today. Your train threads past Tours — TOOR — the valley's capital, and here's a connection to file: Tours matters in this episode's story because it's where your brand-new high-speed line begins. Everything from here to Bordeaux is the 2017 track — from this point on you're on the fast, silent, surprise-free road the whole way south. Settle in; the driver just got their favorite part.

South of the Loire the country changes texture — hedgerows, white cattle, goats — and you enter one of my favorite pieces of invisible geography in Europe: the seuil du Poitou, the Threshold of Poitou. Here's the idea. France has two great masses of ancient hard rock — the Armorican massif under Brittany to the northwest, and the Massif Central to the southeast, the old volcanic highlands you met in episode one as the source of the Dordogne's rivers. Between them runs a low saddle of soft limestone maybe a hundred kilometers wide, connecting the Paris Basin to the Aquitaine Basin. That saddle is the seuil, the threshold — and it is the only easy way to walk, march, ride, or lay track between northern France and the southwest. Every road, every pilgrim route, every railway, every army for two thousand years has funneled through this gap. Your train is in the funnel right now.

And when all of history funnels through one gap, the gap collects battles the way a doorway collects draft. Two of the biggest, both within a day's walk of your track. In 732, near Tours and Poitiers, Charles Martel's Frankish line stopped the great Umayyad raid from Spain — the battle that, in the grand old schoolbook version, decided that Europe north of the Pyrenees would be Christian rather than Muslim; modern historians shrink it to a large raid repulsed, but its legend built kings, and the legend was born in this gap. And in 1356 — here's your week clicking into place — the Black Prince, Edward, Prince of Wales, son of the English king, was caught near Poitiers at the end of one of his great mounted raids, the chevauchées — shuh-voh-SHAY — out of English Aquitaine. Outnumbered, he stood, and shattered the French army, and captured the King of France himself, John the Second. A king of France, taken prisoner in a field you're about to pass at three hundred kilometers an hour. The ransom demanded was so vast it broke French finances for a generation, and the war it belonged to is the same Hundred Years' War whose castles you'll stare across the river at on Sunday afternoon, and whose final cannonade ends ten kilometers from your Thursday wine stop. The raids that made the Black Prince infamous were launched from, and aimed at, precisely the country you'll spend your week in. Same war, seen twice: today from the train at the gap where a king was caught; Sunday from the ramparts where the border actually ground.

Around Poitiers, if you're on the left side, you may glimpse something that looks like a crashed spaceship farm: the Futuroscope theme park, France's 1980s bet on a technology-of-the-image amusement park, still going, still gloriously odd — IMAX domes and mirrored cubes in the middle of goat country. The goats deserve their own sentence, actually: Poitou is chèvre country, and its little cylinder of chabichou — shah-bee-SHOO — is one of the great goat cheeses; if the bar car offers anything with local cheese, that's the province you'd be tasting.

Next waypoint: Angoulême — ahn-goo-LEM — which announces itself, if you catch it, as a walled old town on a dramatic ridge above the Charente river. Angoulême's modern claim is wonderful: it is the comic-book capital of France — home of the great annual comics festival and the national comics museum, the walls around town painted with murals. I enjoy that your route today runs from the caves' painted walls to a city of painted walls; France has been drawing on its buildings for twenty thousand years and sees no reason to stop.

And the Charente — shah-RONT — the slow green river below Angoulême's ridge, is your cue that you've entered spirit country, because downstream lies the town of Cognac. Since you'll be toasting at a wedding in a few days, here's the two-minute science of the world's most famous brandy. Cognac starts as a thin, sharp, frankly unpleasant white wine — and that's the point: high acid and low alcohol survive distillation beautifully. It's distilled twice in onion-shaped copper pot stills — copper matters, it binds the sulfurous off-notes — and the clear spirit then sleeps in barrels of French oak for years to decades. And here's the detail I save for people I like: the evaporation. Around two percent of every barrel is lost to the air every year — the angels' share, la part des anges. Multiply by a region full of warehouses and Cognac loses the equivalent of millions of bottles a year to the sky. The escaping alcohol feeds a black fungus, Baudoinia, that stains the town's rooftops and walls sooty dark — you can literally see where the angels have been drinking. The tax authorities of the eighteenth century learned to spot hidden stills by the blackened walls; the fungus was the informer.

Somewhere in this stretch, glance at the window frame itself and appreciate what you're not feeling. You're crossing river valleys on kilometer-long viaducts and passing under hills in cuttings deep enough to bury a cathedral. The Sud Europe Atlantique line has hundreds of engineered structures; the crossing of the Dordogne itself, east of Bordeaux, is one of the longest viaducts on the line. The train doesn't slow for any of it. Great infrastructure works by making itself unnoticeable — the better the engineering, the less there is to feel — which makes it the exact opposite of a medieval castle, whose entire job was to be felt from ten kilometers away. You'll get to compare directly tomorrow.

After Angoulême the land relaxes, softens, and starts rehearsing the south: the first parasol pines, walls going whitewashed, roofs flattening toward terracotta, and then — vines. When rows of vines start ribboning past in earnest, you've crossed into the Gironde — zhee-ROND — the department of Bordeaux, named for the great funnel estuary where your destination city sits. And in mid-September, look closely at those vines, because you may catch the vendange — vahn-DAHNZH — the grape harvest, the one week of the year when the vineyards are full of people, trailers, and crates. Harvest dates here typically run from mid-September for the merlot; your timing is close to perfect. If you see picking crews from the train today, remember them Thursday in Saint-Émilion, where I'll explain — in that episode — exactly why the merlot comes off the vine first and what the limestone under the town has to do with your glass.

While the Charente country rolls by, two more things about this machine and this line, because they complete the picture.

First, the human system. You're likely riding an Océane trainset — the double-decker generation introduced for this line's 2017 opening; if you're on the upper deck, you're sitting roughly four meters off the rails doing three hundred plus, which is worth savoring for a second. Up front, the driver is doing something counterintuitive: at this speed, human eyes are useless for reading signals beside the track — at eighty-three meters a second, a trackside sign is gone before it registers. So on the high-speed line there are no signals to see; the signaling comes to the driver, displayed in the cab, transmitted through the rails themselves, with the permitted speed continuously shown and automatically enforced. The driver's job at cruise is monitoring, anticipation, and energy management — knowing the line's every rise and dip and letting momentum do the work, surfing a four-hundred-tonne train on gravity between power notches. It's closer to piloting than driving, and France treats it accordingly.

Second, the deep view out the window. Since the first plane episode you've known that France is mostly vanished seas — here's the version you can watch at speed. This whole run from the Loire to Bordeaux is one long slide across sedimentary country: chalk and soft limestone through Poitou — the same broad family of rock, of similar marine vintage, as the cliffs waiting for you in the Périgord; then younger and younger cover as you drop southwest into the Aquitaine Basin proper, until around Bordeaux you're on gravels the rivers dumped practically yesterday, geologically speaking — and river gravels, drained and sun-warmed, are precisely what some of the region's most famous vineyards grow on. The sequence out your window — old chalk to young gravel — is the same staircase you'll climb in reverse on Sunday, driving east and backward in time into older stone and older stories. France arranged its geology very considerately for narrative purposes.

And a small ritual to watch for: the moment another TGV passes the opposite way. Closing speed, around six hundred kilometers an hour; the whole event — twelve carriages of it — lasts about a second and hits your window like a soft punch of air. The nineteenth century would have called that impossible. The French call it Tuesday.

Then the line bends west, joins the old railway, and does something trains have done since 1860: it crosses the Garonne — gah-RON — into Bordeaux on the alignment of the Passerelle Eiffel. The original iron bridge here was an early triumph of a twenty-something engineer named Gustave Eiffel — five hundred meters of iron lattice on deep-sunk piers, the project that helped make his name a decade before the tower made it immortal. The modern crossing beside it carries you into the great arched trainshed of Gare Saint-Jean — gar san-ZHAHN — whose vast iron-and-glass hall, from the same golden age of railway confidence, has just one instruction attached: as you get off, look up.

And since the city at the end of this line is the world's most famous wine name, here's the Bordeaux wine story in miniature — the left-bank half; Saint-Émilion, the right bank, gets its own episode on Thursday.

Why here at all? Because Bordeaux is a port first and a vineyard second. The estuary gave the wine a door to the sea, and the customer walked through it in 1152 — you know her: Eleanor. The moment Aquitaine married England, Bordeaux became England's wine cellar, and for three centuries the autumn wine fleet — hundreds of ships — carried the young light red the English called claret. England drank Bordeaux for so long and so faithfully that the taste outlived the political marriage by five centuries and counting. Then, in the sixteen hundreds, Dutch engineers — the other great hydraulic people — drained the marshes north of the city, exposing those warm gravel banks I mentioned, and the grand estates of the Médoc planted onto them. By 1855, when Napoleon the Third wanted a ranking of the best estates for the Paris Exposition, the Bordeaux brokers produced in two weeks a five-tier classification of the leading châteaux — essentially a price list dressed as a league table — which promptly froze into scripture: it has been revised exactly once in a hundred seventy years. Wine people can recite it the way sports fans recite lineups.

The science, in one glass: Bordeaux is a blending region. The two banks split the work — Cabernet Sauvignon leads on the left bank's warm gravels, which it needs to ripen its thick, tannic skins; Merlot leads on the right bank's cooler limestone and clay, where you're going Thursday. Blending isn't compromise, it's insurance and architecture: the grapes ripen at different times and shine in different vintages, so the blend smooths the weather and layers the structure — Cabernet for the tannic spine and the decades of aging, Merlot for the flesh. And the tannins that make young Bordeaux taste like licking a plank are the whole point: they're antioxidant scaffolding, and as they slowly polymerize over years in bottle they soften, drop, and leave behind the resolved, autumnal thing that made the region's name. Bordeaux is wine engineered on the TGV principle — built as a system, designed for the long run, indifferent to instant gratification. When someone at the wedding this week asks what makes Bordeaux Bordeaux, you now have a better answer than "France."

One face to attach to the city before you arrive, and he's a face you half-know already from the plane episodes: Michel de Montaigne — mon-TEN-yuh. His best friend was Étienne de La Boétie of Sarlat, the teenager who invented civil disobedience — you'll walk past his birthplace on Monday morning — and when La Boétie died young, Montaigne's grief eventually turned into the Essays: the book where a human being first sat down and honestly described his own mind wandering, inventing the personal essay and arguably the modern interior self, under a made-up word — essais, attempts. What's less known is that Montaigne wrote it all a day's ride from your route: his tower still stands on his family estate in the hills between Bordeaux and the Dordogne valley, its beams carved with his favorite quotations, and he served — twice, reluctantly, and by most accounts well — as mayor of Bordeaux, doing his civic duty through plague years and religious war while insisting the whole time that he'd rather be reading. The southwest's two great gifts to philosophy were a Sarlat teenager who said no one can rule you without your consent, and his best friend from Bordeaux who said know what your own mind is actually doing. Between the two of them they roughly bracket the operating manual for a free person, and you'll cross both their doorsteps inside twenty-four hours this week.

And one thing you won't see but should know is out there: past Bordeaux, the river becomes the Gironde estuary — the largest estuary in western Europe, the funnel through which every barrel of claret ever shipped went to sea. At its mouth, on a tidal rock, stands the Cordouan lighthouse — four hundred years old, a Renaissance palace of a lighthouse with a royal apartment and a chapel inside, still manned by keepers, the last inhabited lighthouse in France, and lately given the same UNESCO status as the caves you're about to visit. The French built their lighthouse like a cathedral for the same reason they built their trains like arguments and their replicas like resurrections: the national conviction, running from Lascaux's scaffolding to this seat you're sitting in, that engineering done beautifully enough becomes culture. That's the thread of your whole week, by the way. I'd hold onto it.

So: Bordeaux. You have tonight and an early night — I've seen the itinerary; you have a seven a.m. date with a rental car — so here's the landing briefing, city edition, calibrated for one good evening.

First, the shape of the place. Bordeaux sits on the inside of a crescent bend of the Garonne, which is why its old name is the Port de la Lune, the Port of the Moon — there's a crescent moon on the city's coat of arms. What makes the city visually astonishing — and UNESCO-listed on a nearly unmatched scale; almost half the city is protected — is its unity: mile after mile of matched eighteenth-century golden limestone, because Bordeaux had its boom in one sustained burst in the seventeen hundreds and built accordingly. Honesty clause, and then we'll enjoy the architecture: that gorgeous wealth came from Atlantic trade, and Atlantic trade in the eighteenth century meant wine and colonial sugar and coffee — and Bordeaux was also one of France's major slave-trade ports. The city has, late and imperfectly but genuinely, begun saying so out loud, with plaques and museum rooms naming what the facades were built on. It's worth knowing both truths as you walk the riverfront: it is beautiful, and it was paid for in more than wine.

For your evening, one walk assembles the whole city. Start at the Place de la Bourse, the royal square on the river — pure seventeen-thirties confidence — and cross to the miroir d'eau, the water mirror: the world's largest reflecting pool, two centimeters of water over black granite, cycling between mirror-still and knee-high fog every quarter hour. It sounds like a gimmick; it is in fact the best piece of public space built in Europe this century, and at dusk, when the square's facades double themselves in the water and swifts hunt through the fog jets, you will take the photograph that ends up framed. Then walk the riverfront quays, glance at the Pont de Pierre — the stone bridge Napoleon ordered, with, the story goes, an arch for every letter of his name — and drift back into the old town toward dinner through the Saint-Pierre quarter's narrow streets. If you have room after dinner, find a canelé — kah-nuh-LAY — Bordeaux's own pastry: a small fluted cylinder, mahogany-dark chewy caramelized crust around a custardy rum-and-vanilla heart. Legend credits convent kitchens using up the egg yolks left over when wineries took the whites for clarifying wine — the fining process — and whether or not that's tidy history, it's good eating, and it's the correct dessert for tonight because it's made of exactly this city: wine trade, sugar trade, and gold-brown stone, in pastry form.

Logistics postscript for the morning, since I know the schedule: the Sixt office is on Cours Georges Clemenceau near Place Gambetta — an easy walk from a city-center hotel — pickup at seven, and your itinerary already flags the two truths of the morning: allow twenty minutes for paperwork, and fill the tank before leaving the city, because rural French fuel stations on a Sunday are automated card-readers that sulk at some foreign cards. # Episode 5 — Saint-Cyprien Market
*Sunday ~09:30, playing as you come down into the valley. ~5 min.*

**SCRIPT**

Good morning, you two. If this is playing on schedule, you've survived the Sixt paperwork, the tank is full — it is full, right? — and you're dropping out of the hills into the Dordogne valley for the first time. First stop, deliberately, is not a castle or a cave. It's groceries. Saint-Cyprien — san-see-pree-AN — Sunday market, and it's the right first stop, because the weekly market is the oldest living institution in the region and the fastest way to stop being a visitor and start being a participant.

Saint-Cyprien itself is a proper working town, not a postcard — which is exactly why its market is good. It grew up around a big abbey; you'll see the heavy church tower above the rooftops, more fortress than steeple, which in this region, after episode three, should read as entirely reasonable. # Episode 6 — La Roque-Gageac, the Village in the Cliff
*Sunday ~11:15, on the 25-minute drive from the market. ~5 min.*

**SCRIPT**

You're now driving one of the great short river roads of Europe, and the thing you're about to see tends to make drivers stop paying attention, so Emily, whoever isn't driving is on scenery duty.

La Roque-Gageac — lah ROCK gah-ZHACK — is a single line of golden houses pressed between the Dordogne river and a hundred-meter overhanging cliff, facing due south across the water. It's officially listed among the most beautiful villages in France, and it's one of the few places on the list that actually under-promises. The whole village is maybe one street deep. There was nowhere else to build. They built anyway, for about a thousand years.

Why here? Everything from episode one, cashed in at once. The cliff is a south-facing limestone wall, so the village sits in a natural solar oven — the rock drinks sun all day and radiates it back all night. The microclimate is so gentle that halfway along the lane you'll walk into a little exotic garden — palms, bananas, bamboo, oleander — planted by a local enthusiast in the twentieth century and thriving, absurdly, at the same latitude as Newfoundland. And high up in the cliff face, look for the doorways and galleries cut into the bare rock: the troglodyte fort, a defensive refuge carved in the middle ages, reachable then only by ladders that could be pulled up behind you. Vikings, English routiers, Protestant armies — the threat changed costume every century; the answer stayed the same: go up the wall. People sheltering in this exact limestone is a habit fifty-five thousand years old around here; this village is simply its most photogenic recent chapter.

The river itself is the other half of the story. Before rail, the Dordogne was the highway, and La Roque-Gageac was a port. The flat-bottomed wooden boats you'll see on the water — gabarres, gah-BAR — are modern tourist rebuilds of the barges that ran oak timber, walnut oil, and wine down to Bordeaux. The one-way economics were brutal and wonderful: the current took you down in days, but coming back up meant teams of men and oxen hauling from the bank, so many gabarres were simply sold as lumber in Bordeaux along with the cargo, and the crews walked home. Every barge a one-way ticket; imagine the state of their shoes.

Your plan is forty-five minutes on foot, and it's the right amount: the river path for the postcard view, then the narrow lanes that stair-step up between the houses, find the exotic garden, look up at the fort. Your itinerary says skip the boat ride, and your itinerary is correct — the village is the thing to see, and you see it from the land; from the boat you mostly see other boats. Two footnotes as you walk. One: the cliff gives as well as takes — in 1957 a slab peeled off the overhang and destroyed several houses, killing three people; the scar is still visible, and it's why some upper galleries are closed. Respect any barriers; this is living geology, not a stage set. Two: hold the thought of that one-street, cliff-backed layout — in about three hours, from the ramparts at Beynac, you'll see this whole village again from above, the size of a bracelet on the riverbank, and it's arguably its best angle.

Then lunch. You've earned the terrace. Order something with cèpes if the blackboard offers it — you know why.

# Episode 7 — The Hanging Gardens of Marqueyssac
*Sunday ~13:50, the short hop after lunch. ~5 min.*

**SCRIPT**

Five minutes up the road, and you're swapping raw geology for geology in a tuxedo. The Jardins de Marqueyssac — mar-kay-SACK — are the gardens of a small seventeenth-century château on a spur of rock a hundred thirty meters above the river, and they hold the single best viewpoint over everything you've seen and are about to see today. But the reason to come is stranger than a view: it's a hundred and fifty thousand boxwood trees, clipped by hand into a rolling sea of green domes, and the story of one man's magnificent fixation.

The short history: the estate is old, but the garden as you'll see it is the work of Julien de Cerval, a gentleman who inherited it in the eighteen-sixties after soldiering in Italy, where he'd fallen in love with Italian gardens and, apparently, with boxwood specifically. He spent thirty years planting and clipping his clifftop into something between a garden, a sculpture, and a hallucination — not the rigid geometry of Versailles, but soft, bulbous, cloud-like masses that ripple away over the ridge. After a century of neglect, the whole thing was restored in the nineteen-nineties and reopened in 1997, and today a small team of gardeners still clips every dome by hand, twice a year, with electric shears and infinite patience. If you ever wanted proof that topiary can be an art form rather than a garden trick, this hill is the proof.

Why boxwood, if you want the botany: box is slow, dense, and shade-tolerant, with tiny leaves that regrow from old wood — meaning it takes a haircut like no other plant and holds the shape for months. It's the marble of hedges. Also — you'll notice within a minute — it has a smell: warm, musky, catty, entirely distinctive. People either love it or wrinkle their noses. Either way it's the official smell of aristocratic patience, and you'll carry it out on your clothes. A darker botanical footnote: across Europe, box is being eaten alive by an invasive Asian moth, and gardens like this one now fight a permanent quiet war of traps and treatments to keep a hundred fifty thousand trees alive. The serenity is defended.

Now, your marching orders, and this is the itinerary's best single tip so I'll repeat it with feeling: do not stop at the main belvedere. The crowds pool at the first great viewpoint near the château; the path along the cliff keeps going, and it keeps getting better — quieter, wilder, with the smaller rustic allées, until you reach the far belvedere hanging directly over the river. From up there, the whole morning assembles itself like a map: La Roque-Gageac directly below, a line of gold on the green water; Castelnaud — the English castle — on its hill; the river doing its huge lazy meanders; and across the valley, the fortress you're storming next: Beynac. This terrace is the best place in the Périgord to see the Hundred Years' War as geography — every castle in sight was once a piece on this exact board, in sight of its enemies, because sight was the point.

Housekeeping: it's a two-hour visit at your pace, the paths are proper gravel-and-root walking so the good shoes matter, there's an electric shuttle if feet rebel, and there are peacocks, who own the place and know it. The tearoom is decent if lunch was light. And keep one eye on the clock — your only hard gate today is Beynac's last entry at six, and Marqueyssac is the accordion in the plan: it compresses gracefully. The far belvedere first, then wander back; that way if time runs short, you've already banked the best of it.

# Episode 8 — Beynac, the Angry Castle

Coffee in the village if you're ahead. Next episode plays on that five-minute hop, and it's the one I'd most like you to hear twice.

# Episode 16 — Font-de-Gaume
*Tuesday ~10:45, on the approach and in the queue. ~6 min.*

**SCRIPT**

Here we are. Of everything this week, this is the hour I'd trade for any other. Checklist first, briefly, then how to see it — because seeing Font-de-Gaume well is a genuine skill, and you can learn it in the next five minutes.

Checklist: ticket office by eleven for your eleven-fifteen slot. No bags — none, no lockers, so everything stays at the house or in the car. A layer each: fourteen degrees inside. No photos, and the approach is four hundred meters of steep path, so let the legs arrive warm. That's it.

Now. What you're about to do, plainly stated: you will walk into a hill in single file with about ten other people and a guide, along a passage in places narrower than your shoulders, and you will stand in front of multi-colored paintings — bison, horses, reindeer, mammoths — made by hand, on this rock, in this dark, somewhere around fourteen thousand years ago. Not a replica, not a projection. The last cave on earth where you can still do this with polychrome paintings — everything else of this class is sealed or was never opened. A few dozen people a day, numbers cut year over year for conservation, and no promise the door stays open another decade. Yesterday you saw a perfect copy of a lost original. Today is the original. That's the design of your week, and this is where it pays off.

The seeing lesson, because this is where Font-de-Gaume differs from yesterday: the art does not leap at you. Fourteen millennia of calcite film and fading have pulled some figures back into the rock, and your guide will find them for you with a flashlight, tracing an outline until — and this is the word every visitor uses — the animal suddenly assembles. Here's how to help it happen. One: let your eyes adapt — no phone glow in the queue, and in the cave look away from the guide's light periodically so your night vision builds. Two: when the light traces, follow the moving beam with your eyes rather than staring at the wall generally; your visual system stitches the contour and then, all at once, hands you the bison whole. It's a genuine perceptual event — closer to developing a photograph in your own head than to viewing a picture — and once a figure has assembled for you it stays assembled, which is why the second look is always better than the first. Three: notice the sculpting — the painters chose walls where the rock already bulged like muscle, and the polychrome shading rides the relief; tilt your head as the light moves and watch the animals go three-dimensional.

What to hope the guide shows you — routes vary with conservation, but the classics: the great frieze of bison, five of them processing along one wall. Horses and mammoths layered through the galleries — over two hundred figures in all, more than eighty of them bison. And, if you're lucky, the scene that has quietly become one of the most famous images of the entire Ice Age: two reindeer face to face, antlers curved around each other, the male bending to lick the forehead of the kneeling female. Fourteen thousand years old, and it is unmistakably, deliberately, a picture of tenderness. Whatever the caves were for — magic, cosmology, memory, all of the above — someone in this hill spent hours of lamplight and hard-won pigment recording that one animal loved another. Two days before your friends' wedding, in the dark, on original stone, that's the image you two get to stand in front of. I'd let that be the trip's center of gravity, exactly as your itinerary predicted.

Two last notes. Be good guests of the cave: no touching the walls, ever — skin oils are chemistry, and you know from Lascaux what visitor chemistry does; stay on the walkway, speak low, and give the guide your questions — they're passionate, and the ones here have watched people gasp for years and still love the moment. And when you come out into the September light, don't rush the transition; there's a bench, and the valley below is the same valley the painters saw, minus the reindeer herds. Lunch is booked down the hill, the afternoon is slack, and the only thing on today's schedule after this is a welcome dinner for a wedding. You'll walk into it carrying the licking reindeer. That's the correct order of ceremonies.

# Episode 17 — Saint-Émilion, Where the War Ended
*Thursday ~12:30, on the A89 west. ~6 min.*

**SCRIPT**

Last full episode, and the trip saves a whole genre for the exit: wine. You're driving out of the Périgord the way the gabarres went — downstream, west, toward Bordeaux — and stopping at Saint-Émilion, san-tay-mee-LYON, a golden medieval town on a limestone hill that happens to produce some of the most celebrated red wine on earth. Weddings behind you, one afternoon in hand: here's the briefing.

Start with the hermit, because the town is genuinely named after a guy. Émilion was an eighth-century Breton monk who wanted out of the world and picked a cave in this hillside to want it in. Miracles were reported, disciples gathered, and after his death the community did the most Périgord-adjacent thing imaginable: rather than build a church on the rock, they dug the church out of the rock. Between roughly the ninth and twelfth centuries they hollowed a full monolithic church — mo-no-LEE-teek, one single stone — out of the hill: nave, vaults, windows, all carved downward into living limestone, the largest underground church in Europe, with the bell tower built on top of the hill so it rings above a sanctuary buried beneath the street. You can stand in the square by the bell tower and be standing on the roof of the church. Take the short guided tour if the timing works — it includes the hermit's cave and the catacombs — and if not, the town above ground is compensation enough.

Now the wine, in three moves — and notice it's the same protagonist as all week. Move one: the rock. Saint-Émilion's plateau is limestone — a cousin of the stuff your whole trip is carved from — over clay. For vines this is close to ideal: the roots drive deep, the limestone holds a water reserve and meters it out through dry spells, the clay keeps the vine from ever gorging. Stressed just enough, the vine puts its effort into fewer, more concentrated grapes. Great terroir is mostly well-managed hardship — a sentence with obvious wedding-toast potential, do with it as you see fit. Move two: the grape. This is Merlot country, blended with Cabernet Franc — unlike the Cabernet-led left bank across the river — which makes Saint-Émilion the plusher, rounder, earlier-drinking face of Bordeaux. And Merlot ripens early, which is why your timing matters: mid-September is harvest. Look at the vines as you come in — if crews and crates are out, you're watching the vendange, the one week the whole year points at, and the picking around here is still substantially by hand. Move three: the quarries, and this is my favorite. The stone to build the town — and half of grand Bordeaux — was mined out from under the vineyards, leaving something like two hundred kilometers of galleries under the appellation. Cool, humid, dead-stable in temperature — you learned why in episode one — those tunnels are now the wine's cellars. The rock that grows the wine also ages the wine; the hill is a machine with no wasted parts.

History's two footnotes, both earned this week. First: the town has run on ceremony for eight centuries — its wine brotherhood, the Jurade, descends from the council chartered when this was English Aquitaine — remember, Eleanor's dowry — and its red-robed members still proclaim the harvest from the top of the King's Tower each autumn; you're threading between their festivals. Second, the big one: ten kilometers east of town lies Castillon-la-Bataille, where in July 1453 the French artillery ended the Hundred Years' War — the war you watched across the ramparts at Beynac on Sunday. Your week began inside that war's geography and ends, quite literally, at its finish line. When you order a glass this afternoon, you're drinking at the war memorial; the label just says Grand Cru instead.

Practicals: park outside the walls — the center is pedestrian, steep, and cobbled; the lanes are called tertres, TAIR-truh, and they are ankle-testers, so it's the good shoes one last time. Climb or ride up the bell tower area for the view over vineyard rows running to the horizon in September gold. The local sweet is the macaron — not the Parisian sandwich, but the original almond biscuit, baked here to an Ursuline convent recipe since 1620; buy a dozen from one of the old shops, they travel well, and they're the correct offering to bring home to whoever watered your plants... or, frankly, to eat in the car. And keep half an eye on the clock: away by three-thirty keeps the whole Bordeaux landing gentle. One tiny episode left — save it for the city limits.
